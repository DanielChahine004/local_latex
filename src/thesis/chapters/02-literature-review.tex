\section{Historical Development of Positron Emission Tomography}
\topics{one compact narrative, not a chronicle, keeping only the milestones that set up the cost argument: origins (Anderson 1932; Wrenn 1951; Brownell \& Sweet 1953); tomographic rings and reconstruction imported from CT (PETT series, 1970s); FDG (1976); the block detector (Casey \& Nutt 1986) as the cost-defining innovation this thesis's monolithic approach displaces; septa removal and fully-3D; the BGO$\rightarrow$LSO$\rightarrow$SiPM/TOF lineage as a cost-versus-timing arc; long axial field-of-view systems (uEXPLORER, Biograph Quadra) and the sensitivity-for-price trade they embody.}

\paragraph{Origins}
Positrons were discovered in 1932 by Carl Anderson, who observed the tracks of seemingly positively-charged electrons in a cloud chamber and interpreted them as the electron's antimatter counterpart \cite{Anderson1933}.
The medical potential of positron emitters was realised within two decades: Wrenn and colleagues proposed using the back-to-back annihilation photons for coincidence localisation in 1951 \cite{Wrenn1951}, and Brownell and Sweet built one of the first clinical positron scanners in 1953, comprised of a pair of sodium iodide detectors stepped mechanically across the head on a rectilinear gantry \cite{Sweet1953}.
These pre-tomographic instruments produced planar projections with no means of reconstructing depth, but they established coincidence detection of annihilation photons as a route to localising tracer uptake \emph{in vivo}---the principle on which all later PET instrumentation was built.

\paragraph{Inheritance from Single-Photon Imaging}
Two developments in single-photon imaging shaped what followed.
Anger's scintillation camera of 1958 replaced mechanical scanning with a single large NaI(Tl) crystal viewed by an array of photomultiplier tubes, positioning each event from the centroid of the light distribution \cite{Anger1958}---a stationary-detector, position-decoding architecture that PET block detectors would later inherit.
Kuhl and Edwards then demonstrated emission tomography itself in 1963, rotating a scanner about the patient and reconstructing transverse sections from the resulting projections \cite{Kuhl1963}, several years before the equivalent was achieved with transmission X-rays \cite{Hounsfield1973}.
Tomography thus reached PET second-hand: the ring scanners of the 1970s combined coincidence detection, already established, with reconstruction mathematics developed for single-photon and X-ray imaging.

\paragraph{The Move to Tomographic Rings}
In 1961, a group at Brookhaven National Laboratory developed a ring of 32 sodium iodide-based coincidence detectors surrounding the head \cite{Rankowitz2008}.
By the late 1960s Brownell and the group at Massachusetts General Hospital had developed a so-called `hybrid scanner' comprised of two stationary planar 2D arrays of detectors, and by the early 1970s the group had developed a device of similar architecture known as the ``multi-crystal positron camera'', which had a field of view of $\cm{27}\times\cm{30}$ \cite{Burnham2008}.
In the mid-1970s, Ter-Pogossian, Phelps and Hoffman built the PETT series: rings of scintillation detectors in which every crystal was in coincidence with those opposite it, so that a single acquisition sampled the object along lines of response at many angles at once \cite{Ter-Pogossian1975}.
The projections this produced were of exactly the form that filtered backprojection had been developed to invert, and the reconstruction was imported essentially intact from the single-photon and X-ray tomography described above \cite{Phelps1975}.

\paragraph{FDG and Clinical Viability}
The short-lived isotopes of the early years of PET confined imaging to blood flow and oxygen metabolism, scanners were only as useful as the tracers available to them.
The synthesis of \textsuperscript{18}F-fluorodeoxyglucose in 1976 removed that constraint \cite{Ido1978}.
FDG is taken up in proportion to glucose consumption and then trapped intracellularly, so a scan maps metabolic rate directly; the \mins{110} half-life of \textsuperscript{18}F is long enough to permit production at a cyclotron some distance from the scanner.
These two properties made PET highly clinically viable and drove much demand for PET scanners.

\paragraph{The Block Detector}
The block detector, introduced by Casey and Nutt in 1986 \cite{Casey1986}, in response to increasing demand, shifted focus towards cost-effective detector architectures to maximise the clinical utility of PET.
The difficulty it addressed was economic: spatial resolution requires many small detector elements, but coupling each element to its own photomultiplier tube scaled channel count linearly alongside, and therefore the price, scale with resolution.
Casey and Nutt cut a shallow grid of saw-slots into a single block of BGO, dividing it into an array of quasi-independent elements whose light was shared among only four photomultipliers, and identified the element of interaction from the ratios of the four signals---Anger's centroid logic applied at the scale of a few centimetres rather than a whole camera head.
The channel count was decoupled from the crystal count, and with it the dominant term in the cost of resolution.
Virtually every clinical PET scanner built since has used some variant of this arrangement.
In the decades that followed, removing the septa between axially stacked detector rings and acquiring in fully-3D raised sensitivity several-fold at the cost of a much larger scattered fraction, and increasingly necessitated corrective algorithms to resolve it.

\paragraph{Scintillators, Timing and Scale}
In the 1980s, available fast-response scintillators such as BaF2 and CsF successfully demonstrated the advantages of TOF-PET, but they had drawbacks: notably low density and low photofraction, which compromised performance enough for BGO to continue as the dominant PET scintillator \cite{Moses2007}.
LSO in the late 1980s, and LYSO by the early 2000s, delivered the faster decay times that TOF-PET demanded without the density and photofraction drawbacks of predecessor scintillators, effectively reviving the technique and gaining popularity over BGO in spite of their slightly higher price \cite{Moses2007}.
Silicon photomultipliers, developed over the late 1990s and 2000s, had by the early 2010s matured enough to warrant their adoption over traditional photomultiplier tubes, improving timing, compactness, and permitting operation inside a magnetic field.
Most recently, long axial field-of-view systems such as the uEXPLORER and the Biograph Quadra have raised sensitivity by an order of magnitude by extending the axial length of the scanner to cover the whole body \cite{Vandenberghe2020, slhd2021quadra}.

\section{Annihilation-Photon Generation: Physics and Radiotracers}

\subsection{Positron Emission, Annihilation and Coincidence Detection}
\label{sec:annihilation}
\topics{$\beta^+$ decay, thermalisation, annihilation, LORs, timing windows, event types. Two branches the conventional account omits, per Figure~\ref{fig:chain-emission}: positronium (para versus ortho lifetimes, the three-photon channel, lifetime imaging as a live direction) and prompt-gamma emitters (\textsuperscript{68}Ga, \textsuperscript{124}I, \textsuperscript{44}Sc), whose third photon makes a triple coincidence that localises along the LOR without TOF. Both belong here, not in a footnote.}

\paragraph{Positron Emission}
As a nuclear imaging modality, PET employs radioactive materials to achieve functional imaging, and the decay mode it exploits is beta-plus ($\beta^+$) decay: a nucleus with excess protons converts a proton into a neutron, emitting a positron and a neutrino and decreasing the atomic number by one.
Proton-rich nuclei may also reach the same daughter by electron capture, which emits no positron and so contributes nothing to the PET signal; the two routes compete, and the fraction of decays taking the $\beta^+$ branch varies by isotope.
\emph{Positron emission} is what distinguishes PET: the detected photons are not directly emitted by the nucleus of the radioactive source, but are a consequence of the emitted positron subsequently annihilating with an atomic electron.
A positron leaves the nucleus with a continuous spectrum of kinetic energies and does not annihilate immediately.
It first \emph{thermalises}, losing energy through Coulomb scattering with the surrounding medium over a path of length that typically ranges from a fraction of a millimetre to several millimetres (depending on the isotope) before combining with an electron at near-rest.\needcite{Levin \& Hoffman 1999, Phys Med Biol -- positron range by isotope and its limit on PET resolution}
That annihilation converts the rest mass of the particle and anti-particle into two high-energy photons, each of \keV{511} in accordance with the energy--mass equivalence defined by $E=mc^2$.
These are termed \emph{annihilation photons} rather than gamma rays, since they originate outside the nucleus; they are the photons PET detects, and are referred to as such throughout this thesis.
The displacement between the point of emission and the point of annihilation is known as the \emph{positron range}, and because PET reconstructs the latter while the tracer chemistry occurs at the former, it introduces a hard resolution limit that no detector design can recover from (\S\ref{sec:resolution-limits}).

\paragraph{LOR Formation}
Because the annihilating pair is very nearly at rest, conservation of momentum requires that the two photons be emitted almost exactly back-to-back.
The pair is not quite at rest, however, and its residual momentum spreads the opening angle about \degs{180} with a full width at half maximum of roughly \degs{0.5}.\needcite{Colombino et al.\ 1965 for the measurement; or Cherry, Sorenson \& Phelps, Physics in Nuclear Medicine, for the textbook figure}
Detecting both annihilation photons in opposite detector elements therefore constrains the annihilation to have occurred somewhere along the straight line joining them, termed the \emph{line of response} (LOR).
Nothing localises the annihilation along that particular coincidence event's LOR---tomographic reconstruction recovers the source distribution from the intersection of many LORs---though TOF-PET, by resolving the difference in the two photons' arrival times, can additionally estimate where along each LOR the annihilation likely took place.
This coincidence localisation principle differentiates PET from single-photon emission imaging such as gamma cameras and SPECT, where a physical collimator must constrain the direction of photon incidence and discards the vast majority of emitted photons to do so.
PET's coincidence requirement is often described as \emph{electronic collimation} for this reason.

\paragraph{Coincidence Timing}
In practice, the two annihilation photons from a single event are rarely detected at the exact same time.
They travel different distances to the detector ring, penetrate to different depths into the scintillator material, and the readout electronics themselves have finite timing resolution.
A \emph{coincidence timing window} on the order of \qtyrange{4}{12}{\nano\second} is therefore permitted: two photons detected within that timespan of each other are assumed to originate from the same annihilation event, and are paired to form an LOR.\needcite{Bailey et al., Positron Emission Tomography: Basic Sciences; or Cherry, Sorenson \& Phelps -- typical clinical window widths}
Widening the window recovers valid events lost to timing jitter but admits more spurious events, so its width is a direct trade against the event classification that follows.

\paragraph{Event Types}
Not every accepted coincidence carries usable information about the source distribution, which is what reconstruction ultimately needs.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{True coincidences} are the idealised type of event; those in which both photons from a single annihilation reach the detectors unscattered. Only these produce an LOR that genuinely passes through the annihilation point.
	\item \textbf{Scattered coincidences} occur when one or both photons undergo Compton interaction (in the patient or elsewhere) before detection, deflecting them so the assigned LOR no longer intersects the annihilation point.
	\item \textbf{Random coincidences} arise when photons from two unrelated annihilations incidentally fall within the same timing window, producing an LOR unrelated to either event.
	\item \textbf{Multiple coincidences} occur whenever three or more detections fall within the same coincidence timing window. These are conventionally discarded because the correct pairing is ambiguous.
\end{itemize}
Scattered and random events both contribute a slowly varying background that reduces contrast and must be estimated and subtracted during reconstruction (\S\ref{sec:corrections}); their relative magnitude, and the cost of correcting for them, recurs throughout this thesis.

\subsection{Scintillation Mechanisms and Figures of Merit}
\label{sec:scintillation-mechanisms}
\topics{two distinct mechanisms: inorganic scintillation via band structure and activator dopants, organic via molecular $\pi$-electron singlets -- why the families behave so differently at \keV{511}. \paragraph{The Role of the Scintillator}
The scintillator serves a dual-function for gamma detection; inciting interactions from gamma photons, generally by virtue of a high density and high atomic number; and amplification, converting the interaction of a single photon into a shower of lower-energy photons, in quantities that relate to the amount of energy deposited in the gamma interaction, enabling electronic detection and energy discrimination.
Scintillator properties directly impact any given scanner's sensitivity, timing performance, event discrimination, and ultimately spatial resolution.
The ideal scintillator for PET has a high light yield to maximise the SNR, a fast rise time to suppourt accurate timing resolution, fast decay time to support high activity count rates and TOF measurements, a high density to increase the probability of photon interaction, high effective atomic number to maximise the occurance of photoelectric interactions, optical transparency and homogeneity, and an emission spectrum that matches the photodetector's sensitivity.
Additional practical considerations for scintillators relate to their manufacturability, cost, material availability and sustainability, required support infastructure (such as cryogenics or moisture control) and long-term stability, which are crucial for large-scale deployment in clinical systems.

\paragraph{Inorganic Scintillation}
What characterises an inorganic scintillator is that the light-emitting process belongs to the solid rather than to any individual molecule: energy is converted through the delocalised band structure of a wide-band-gap insulator, and the charge carriers must travel through the material before they can emit.
Conversion proceeds in three stages.
A \keV{511} photon interacting photoelectrically or by Compton scatter liberates an energetic electron, which cascades by impact ionisation and Auger decay until the resulting carriers fall below the ionisation threshold and thermalise to the band edges, leaving a dense track of electron--hole pairs.
These carriers then migrate, some binding into excitons and some lost to trapping or non-radiative quenching, until they are captured at luminescence centres -- typically an activator dopant such as Ce\textsuperscript{3+} (as in LSO, LYSO) or Tl\textsuperscript{+} (as in NaI) -- where they recombine radiatively and emit many lower-energy optical photons (visible or near-visible light).
Because the activator levels lie within the band gap, the emitted photons are too low in energy to re-excite the host, and the crystal remains transparent to its own light.
The number of electron--hole pairs created by a deposit of energy $E$ scales as $E/\beta E_\mathrm{g}$, where $E_\mathrm{g}$ is the band gap of the host and $\beta \approx 2$--$3$ is the factor by which the average energy cost of one thermalised pair exceeds that gap, the excess being lost to the phonons emitted during thermalisation.
The band gap therefore sets a hard ceiling on light yield before any transport or quantum-efficiency losses are counted.

\paragraph{Luminescence Transitions and Why They Set the Decay Time}

\paragraph{Organic Scintillation}
Organic scintillators emit by a mechanism belonging to the individual molecule rather than to the bulk solid, which is the property that divides the two families.
The scintillating species is an aromatic hydrocarbon whose delocalised $\pi$-electron system supports a ladder of singlet ($S_0$, $S_1$, $S_2$, \dots) and triplet ($T_1$, \dots) states, each subdivided into vibrational sublevels.
An ionising particle excites $\pi$ electrons into the upper singlets, which relax non-radiatively to $S_1$ by internal conversion within picoseconds; the $S_1 \rightarrow S_0$ transition then fluoresces in \qtyrange{1}{3}{\nano\second}.
That speed is a direct consequence of the mechanism: the transition is both spin- and dipole-allowed, and no charge-transport stage intervenes between excitation and emission.
Because fluorescence terminates on vibrationally excited sublevels of $S_0$, the emitted photon is lower in energy than the $S_0 \rightarrow S_1$ absorption and the material is transparent to its own light, the molecular counterpart of the activator level lying inside the band gap.
A competing path exists: intersystem crossing populates $T_1$, from which radiative decay is spin-forbidden and therefore slow, while bimolecular annihilation of two $T_1$ excitations regenerates $S_1$ and produces delayed fluorescence---the origin of the slow component that pulse-shape discrimination exploits.
Practical plastics are ternary systems in which a polymer base such as polyvinyltoluene absorbs most of the deposited energy and transfers it non-radiatively by F\"orster dipole--dipole coupling to a primary fluor at around one per cent by weight, with a wavelength shifter at a far lower concentration moving the emission to longer wavelengths to match the photodetector and limit self-absorption.
Since the emitting species is the bulk itself rather than a dilute dopant, composition is locked to carbon and hydrogen, and the light yield and effective atomic number cannot be raised by doping as they can in an inorganic host.

\paragraph{Why the Molecular Mechanism Is Low-Yield}
The mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:scintillation-mechanisms} is fast, but it is also intrinsically dim: a polyvinyltoluene-based plastic yields around \num{10000} photons per \unit{\mega\electronvolt} against roughly \num{38000} for NaI(Tl), and the deficit has several independent causes.
Most of the deposited energy is dissipated in ionisation and in vibrational modes of the polymer base that never reach a fluor at all, and what does reach one must survive a two-step transfer chain to the primary fluor and then the wavelength shifter, each step lossy.
Of the excitations that do form by charge recombination, spin statistics favour triplets over singlets three to one, and the triplet branch contributes nothing to the prompt light.
Ionisation quenching removes more: where the local energy-loss rate is high, excited molecules are destroyed by damaged neighbours before they can fluoresce, an effect described empirically by Birks' law,
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\mathrm{d}L}{\mathrm{d}x} = \frac{S \, \mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x}{1 + k_\mathrm{B} \, \mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}x},
	\label{eq:birks}
\end{equation}
in which $L$ is the emitted light, $S$ the scintillation efficiency in the unquenched limit, and $k_\mathrm{B} \approx \qty{0.1}{\milli\metre\per\mega\electronvolt}$ in plastic sets the energy-loss rate above which the response ceases to be proportional.
The consequence for PET is that plastics reach useful timing performance by way of speed rather than photon statistics, and pay for it in energy resolution---the subject of the low effective-$Z$ problem that follows.
