\paragraph{Radiotracers and What PET Measures}
Positron emission tomography (PET) is an emission imaging modality: the quantity it measures is the spatial distribution of a positron-emitting radionuclide inside the subject, reconstructed from the annihilation photons that distribution emits.
This distinguishes PET from transmission modalities, in which an external source is driven through the subject and the flux is measured on the far side.
The consequence is that the signal available to a PET scanner is fixed by the amount of activity the subject can safely be given and by the fraction of the emitted photons the instrument can intercept, rather than by the strength of a source the operator controls; every design decision in the chapters that follow is ultimately an attempt to recover more of that fixed budget of photons.
The radionuclide is bound to a biologically active carrier molecule to form a radiotracer, which is administered to the subject, generally via injection into the bloodstream, and progressively accumulates according to the biological process that the carrier targets.
The most widely used tracer, fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG), is a glucose analogue that is taken up by glucose transporters and trapped after phosphorylation, and therefore accumulates in tissues with high glycolytic activity; other tracers instead target receptor expression, perfusion, or protein deposition.
The choice of carrier determines what the image means but not how it is formed: whatever the tracer, the measurement is the same, since all positron emitters are observed through the identical \keV{511} annihilation signature.

\paragraph{Annihilation and Coincidence Detection}
Over time, the radionuclide decays, emitting positrons, which travel a short distance known as the positron range before annihilating with an electron and producing two approximately collinear \keV{511} annihilation photons, a small fraction of which escape the subject's body without scattering and are detected by the PET scanner.
The positron range depends on the emitter, from sub-millimetre for fluorine-18 to several millimetres for higher-energy emitters such as rubidium-82, and the residual momentum of the electron--positron pair at annihilation spreads the opening angle about \degs{180} with a full width at half maximum of roughly \degs{0.5}; together these place a fundamental limit on the spatial resolution achievable by any conventional PET system, irrespective of detector quality.
Two photons detected within a coincidence window of a few nanoseconds are taken to originate from the same annihilation, and the positions at which they are detected define a line of response (LOR): the path along which reconstruction presumes the annihilation event took place.

\paragraph{From Lines of Response to Images}
As hundreds of millions of coincidence events are recorded over the course of a scan, the resulting dataset of LORs is used, with the aid of tomographic image reconstruction algorithms, to produce a volumetric image of the radiotracer distribution.
In principle, where more LORs intersect in the volume, more annihilation events are indicated, and thus the radiotracer concentration there is higher, and vice versa; this correspondence holds only once the data are corrected for attenuation, scattered and random coincidences, and non-uniform detector sensitivity, which otherwise bias the apparent distribution.
Where the radiotracer appears to accumulate, by proxy we infer that the corresponding biological process targeted by the tracer is occurring.

\paragraph{Functional Imaging and Hybrid Systems}
This makes PET a functional or molecular imaging modality as opposed to an anatomical imaging modality such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which image the anatomical structure of the subject.
However, many modern clinical workflows employ PET in combination with CT or MRI, often in hybrid scanner systems, so that functional and anatomical information are acquired in a single session and spatially co-registered.
In PET/CT the two acquisitions are sequential, whereas PET/MRI permits genuinely simultaneous acquisition.
Beyond informing clinical interpretation, the anatomical scan also supports reconstruction itself: CT measures attenuation directly, but at the effective energy of the X-ray beam rather than at \keV{511}, where the photoelectric contribution is negligible and attenuation is governed almost entirely by electron density; a bilinear rescaling of Hounsfield units is therefore applied to convert the CT image into a \keV{511} attenuation map\needcite{standard reference for bilinear CT-to-511\,keV conversion, e.g. Kinahan or Carney}.
Deriving an equivalent map from MRI data is harder still, since the MRI signal bears no direct relation to attenuation and cortical bone is invisible at conventional echo times; the segmentation-, atlas- and learning-based methods now in clinical use are workable but remain least accurate in exactly the tissues that matter most, bone and lung.

\paragraph{Scanner Anatomy}
A PET measurement is best understood as a chain of successive transductions, in which the signal is repeatedly converted from one physical carrier into another and something is paid at every conversion.
The scanner's detectors are built around this chain: a scintillator converts an incoming annihilation photon into some thousands of optical photons, a photodetector converts those into a charge pulse, and readout electronics amplify, digitise and timestamp that pulse so that pairs of them can be sorted into coincidences and passed to a computer for reconstruction.
Each conversion has both a multiplicity, which determines how much signal survives, and a resolution cost, which determines how well the event can be localised; the design of a scanner is largely the business of choosing where in that chain to spend money.
Figures~\ref{fig:chain-emission} to~\ref{fig:chain-readout} set out that chain in full, from administration to reconstructed image, one stage at a time.
Each stage names first the quantities that govern the conversion---the numbers a designer actually trades against---and then the physical channels available to it: the one a conventional scanner uses, and the alternatives that could in principle serve the same purpose and are not used.
Most of the alternatives are not pursued in this thesis.
They are given because a chain drawn with only its conventional path reads as a description of what PET is, when what it actually records is a long series of choices: localisation, for instance, need not be optical at all, since the energy deposited in a detector also launches a pressure wave whose acoustic time of flight would encode the interaction depth directly, and the scattered photons that conventional PET discards carry, in their scattering angle, the information needed to place the event without a second detector at all.
Some of these alternatives are the basis of active research programmes; others are real effects that are simply too weak to build an instrument on, the thermoacoustic signal from a single \keV{511} deposition being far below the noise floor of any practical transducer at clinical count rates.
Which is which, and why, is taken up in the chapters that follow.
The division between the figures is itself the argument of this thesis in miniature: almost nothing in Figure~\ref{fig:chain-emission} is under the designer's control, since it is fixed by isotope physics and patient anatomy, whereas almost everything in Figures~\ref{fig:chain-optical} and~\ref{fig:chain-readout} is a decision, and therefore a place where money is either spent or saved.

\paragraph{Where the Cost Sits}
The cost of PET scanners divides between several aspects including the cost of scintillator materials, photodetectors, readout electronics, precision fabrication of pixellated arrays, radiotracer supply chain logistics, siting and shielding requirements, and the cost of routine maintenance.
